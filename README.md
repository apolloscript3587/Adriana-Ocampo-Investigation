# Adriana Ocampo Uría — Research Dossier

**Language:** English  
**Research snapshot:** 13 October 2026  
**Subject:** Dr. Adriana C. Ocampo Uría, Colombian-born planetary geologist and space-science leader

This repository documents a source-led investigation into Adriana Ocampo's career, scientific contributions, space-mission leadership, recognitions, public engagement, and current institutional affiliations. It is designed as a living research record: sources are linked directly, claims are qualified when institutional biographies conflict, and the dossier distinguishes scientific contributions from the separate role of managing a mission program.

## Important current-affiliation note

The NASA Science profile still labels Ocampo “Scientist — NASA Headquarters,” but that page says it was last updated on 5 November 2024. The UNESCO COMEST membership directory, updated 25 September 2026, describes her as a former NASA Science Program Manager / former New Frontiers lead program executive, and identifies her as a Courtesy Research Professor in the University of Central Florida Department of Physics and Astronomy, a member of the Colombian Academy of Exact, Physical and Natural Sciences, and Vice-President of UNESCO's World Commission on the Ethics of Scientific Knowledge and Technology (COMEST) for 2026–2027. UCF's own faculty page independently lists her as Courtesy Research Professor. Therefore this research does not state without qualification that she currently works at NASA; the freshest institutional evidence available at the research date points to post-NASA roles while NASA's profile appears stale. See [Current role and source reconciliation](research/current-role.md).

## Research questions and scope

1. **Identity and background:** birth city, upbringing, migration, professional identity, and the importance of her Colombian and Latin American connections.
2. **Education and career path:** formal degrees, early entry into the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL), and changes in responsibility over time.
3. **Earth-impact science:** research on the buried Chicxulub impact structure, Landsat imagery, the cenote ring, field expeditions, and the Aorounga radar-imaging work.
4. **Planetary and space missions:** her documented responsibilities in the New Frontiers program (New Horizons, Juno, OSIRIS-REx), the Discovery mission Lucy, planetary mission coordination at JPL, and Venus exploration collaboration/planning.
5. **Leadership versus mission science:** what it means to serve as a program executive or science-program manager, and which accomplishments should be attributed to Ocampo personally versus large scientific teams.
6. **Awards and distinctions:** documented awards, public-science recognitions, institutional appointments, and the naming of asteroid 177120 Ocampo Uría.
7. **Public engagement and mentorship:** science communication, women in STEM, and Spanish-language educational material.
8. **Evidence quality and open questions:** inconsistent dates/titles, incomplete award documentation, and what should be checked next.

## Key findings

- NASA's official biography says Ocampo was born in **Barranquilla, Colombia**, grew up in Argentina, and moved to the United States as a teenager. It records a B.S. in geology from California State University, Los Angeles (1983) and an M.S. in planetary geology from California State University, Northridge. Her doctoral work was at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam.
- She began her space-science path as a high-school volunteer at JPL and later worked there while studying. Her career included planetary-mission coordination and later science-program leadership.
- Her impact-geology work helped identify and map the **surface expression of the buried Chicxulub impact structure** on Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula using remote sensing and the ring of cenotes. It is more precise to describe this as a collaborative, evidence-building contribution than as the discovery of the crater by one person in isolation.
- Her NASA mission portfolio included high-profile exploration of Pluto and the Kuiper Belt (New Horizons), Jupiter (Juno), and the asteroid Bennu sample-return mission (OSIRIS-REx). NASA mission pages document the spacecraft results; UNESCO's current profile is the source for Ocampo's former program-executive roles.
- Her documented recognitions include the 1992 Woman of the Year in Science award from Comisión Femenil in Los Angeles, a 1996 JPL Advisory Council for Women Award, the 1997 Science and Technology Award from the Chicano Federation, inclusion in Discover magazine's 2002 “50 Most Important Women in Science” feature, and the 2022 Latin America Lifetime Award reported by NASA en Español. Asteroid **177120 Ocampo Uría** was named in her honor.
- The career is notable not only for spaceflight management, but for the combination of field geology, satellite/radar-image interpretation, planetary-science program leadership, international cooperation, and science outreach.

## Evidence standard

**Primary sources** (NASA, JPL, UNESCO, UCF, peer-reviewed papers, and university repositories) take priority. Secondary biographies are used for leads and corroboration, not to override stronger institutional records. Dates and current titles are time-sensitive. When biographies disagree, the dossier preserves the discrepancy and records each source's update context. Missions are described with a distinction between scientific findings produced by mission teams and Ocampo's management or coordination responsibilities.

## Citation style

Inline links are used throughout the Markdown files. The bibliography records the organization/author, page or paper title, date when available, source type, URL, and the claims for which the source is useful. Access and content checked through 13 October 2026.
