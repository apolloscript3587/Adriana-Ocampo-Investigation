# Scientific Contributions and Publications

## Overview

Dr. Adriana Ocampo's scientific work is especially associated with impact geology, remote sensing, and understanding the geological traces left by impacts on Earth. She is also a planetary scientist whose program-management career helped shape robotic exploration missions. The scientific publications and the mission-management record should be read together but not treated as the same type of contribution.

## 1. Chicxulub: mapping the cenote ring

The buried Chicxulub impact structure lies beneath the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico and is strongly associated with the end-Cretaceous mass extinction. Because the crater is buried under later sediments, its surface expression is subtle. One of the most visible patterns is a ring of sinkholes, known locally as cenotes. The 1993 paper by Kevin O. Pope, Adriana C. Ocampo, and Charles E. Duller describes how Landsat imagery was used to map the cenote ring and how the pattern demarcated a boundary related to the buried impact structure.

The importance of this research is methodological as well as geological: satellite observations can reveal the large-scale surface expression of a buried impact structure, while subsurface geology and field evidence are needed to test and interpret the pattern. It is an example of combining remote sensing with field and geological reasoning.

**Key peer-reviewed reference**

Pope, K. O., Ocampo, A. C., & Duller, C. E. (1993). “Surficial geology of the Chicxulub impact crater, Yucatan, Mexico.” *Earth, Moon, and Planets*, 63, 93–104. [DOI: 10.1007/BF00575099](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00575099).

The paper names three authors. Accordingly, this research describes Ocampo as an important contributor to mapping and interpreting the surface geology, rather than giving her sole credit for the discovery. NASA's own biography says research led by Ocampo using satellite imagery contributed to discovery of the crater, but the contemporary paper makes the collaborative character of the work clear.

Additional source: [NASA Science biography](https://science.nasa.gov/people/adriana-ocampo/).

## 2. Later analysis of the crater's surface expression

A later study by Ocampo and colleagues, indexed by PubMed, analyzed geomorphic, soil, and topographic evidence from the northern Yucatán Peninsula. The published abstract describes concentric features in the topography, a trough containing cenotes, and the relationship of the surface pattern to ring faults and the buried crater's structure. The study supports a crater basin interpretation with a diameter around 180 km, while the concentric surface features extend well beyond the principal basin.

Reference: [“Surface expression of the Chicxulub crater,” Geology (1996), PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11539331/).

## 3. Doctoral research: impact ejecta and Chicxulub deposits

The Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam research portal catalogs Ocampo's PhD thesis as:

*Geology and Emplacement Mechanism of Chicxulub Crater Deposits: An Analogue for Planetary Impact Ejecta.*

The institutional record assigns the thesis to the PhD-thesis category and gives an award date of 25 March 2013. Its subject connects the geology of the Chicxulub impact structure to the broader planetary-science problem of how impact ejecta is distributed and emplaced. Impact structures on Earth can act as natural laboratories for interpreting craters on other worlds, where direct access to subsurface samples is often limited.

Source: [Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam thesis record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/geology-and-emplacement-mechanism-of-chicxulub-crater-deposits-an/).

## 4. Aorounga and spaceborne radar

In March 1996, NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory published a report about radar observations of northern Chad collected by the Spaceborne Imaging Radar-C/X-SAR instruments flown on Space Shuttle Endeavour in 1994. The analysis suggested that Aorounga might be part of a group of two or more impact craters arranged in an arc. Ocampo was quoted in the JPL report and co-authored a research contribution titled “Shuttle Imaging Radar (SIR-C) Images Reveal Multiple Impact Craters at Aorounga, Northern Chad.”

The case is useful for understanding how planetary geology methods are applied to Earth: radar penetrates some surface-cover limitations and can reveal geomorphology not obvious in ordinary visible imagery. However, the JPL report also preserves an important uncertainty: additional candidate craters lacked field confirmation at the time, and scientists differed on whether all features should be accepted as impact structures. A rigorous biography should not convert a proposed identification into a definitive result.

## 5. Research methods represented in this work

The sourced work shows a combination of:
- Satellite-image interpretation, including Landsat data.
- Spaceborne radar-image interpretation from the SIR-C/X-SAR mission.
- Geomorphology and topographic analysis.
- Field expeditions and comparison of surface observations with buried impact structures.
- Geological interpretation of deposits and impact ejecta.

The NASA biography says Ocampo led six research expeditions to study Chicxulub. It does not enumerate dates and expedition teams on the profile, so six should be cited as NASA's biographical statement, not treated as an independently reconstructed count.

## 6. From impact geology to planetary science

Impact geology links Earth's history with planetary evolution. The same physical processes that create craters on Earth occur across the Solar System, but Earth's erosion, tectonics, vegetation, sedimentation and oceans can hide or alter impact structures. A research career based on Earth-based remote sensing and impact ejecta provides a useful foundation for a planetary geologist working with spacecraft images and planetary-science programs.

This relationship offers one explanation for how Ocampo's research background complements her work around robotic missions. The dossier does not claim that the Chicxulub studies directly produced a particular spacecraft instrument or mission result; instead, it treats them as connected areas of expertise within planetary science.
