# Biography and Career Overview

**Research date:** 13 October 2026  
**Name used by institutions:** Adriana C. Ocampo Uría; often credited as Adriana Ocampo  
**Field:** Planetary geology, impact geology, planetary mission management, science policy and outreach

## Summary

Dr. Adriana C. Ocampo Uría is a planetary geologist born in Barranquilla, Colombia, whose career connects Earth impact geology with robotic exploration of the Solar System. NASA's biography says she grew up in Argentina and moved to the United States as a teenager. She entered the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) as a high-school volunteer and subsequently pursued geology while working at JPL. Her career grew from technical and scientific work around planetary missions into science-program leadership at NASA Headquarters. In later institutional listings, she is identified as a Courtesy Research Professor at the University of Central Florida (UCF), founder and president of the Stelpolvo Ocampo Uria Foundation, and Vice-President of UNESCO's World Commission on the Ethics of Scientific Knowledge and Technology (COMEST) for 2026–2027. The role history is addressed separately because older NASA pages still list a NASA Headquarters job title.

Sources: [NASA Science biography](https://science.nasa.gov/people/adriana-ocampo/); [UNESCO COMEST members, updated 25 September 2026](https://www.unesco.org/fr/ethics-science-technology/comest?hl=es-PE); [UCF faculty profile](https://sciences.ucf.edu/physics/person/adriana-c-ocampo-uria/).

## Early life and identity

Ocampo was born in Barranquilla, on Colombia's Caribbean coast. Her family moved to Argentina when she was young, and then to the United States during her teenage years. Her own account on NASA's site describes looking at the stars from the roof of her home in Argentina and improvising toy spacecraft. NASA's account presents her parents as major sources of encouragement and emphasizes persistence and education.

This transnational background is relevant to the public significance of her career: she is a Colombian-born scientist whose education and professional life span Latin America, the United States, and European/international science institutions. It is more precise to describe her as Colombian-born and internationally educated than to make unsupported assumptions about citizenship or nationality beyond the wording of reliable biographies.

**Primary source:** [NASA Science — Adriana Ocampo](https://science.nasa.gov/people/adriana-ocampo/).

## Education

- **B.S. in Geology**, California State University, Los Angeles, awarded in 1983, according to NASA's biography.
- **M.S. in Planetary Geology**, California State University, Northridge. NASA says she completed this degree while working full-time at JPL; the official NASA page does not display a degree date.
- **Doctoral research in planetary/impact geology**, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam. The university repository catalogs her thesis as *Geology and Emplacement Mechanism of Chicxulub Crater Deposits: An Analogue for Planetary Impact Ejecta* and records the thesis as a PhD thesis dated 25 March 2013.

The thesis record and other biographies do not always use identical years for the doctoral process. This dossier relies on the university's research record for the bibliographic date and avoids asserting a more precise graduation timeline without another official academic record.

Sources: [NASA biography](https://science.nasa.gov/people/adriana-ocampo/); [Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam thesis record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/geology-and-emplacement-mechanism-of-chicxulub-crater-deposits-an/).

## Early career at JPL

Ocampo began at JPL through Space Exploration Post 509, a program sponsored by JPL. NASA says she first volunteered there after her junior year in high school, then worked at JPL during the summer and continued while studying at university. UNESCO's 2026 profile summarizes her prior position as a planetary-mission science coordinator at JPL and names missions including Viking, Mars Observer, Voyager, and Galileo.

Her early career is important because it was not limited to policy or executive oversight. It developed in a technical mission environment where image processing, instrument work, mission operations, and the interpretation of planetary data connect engineering systems to science results. The available official sources confirm scientific coordination, though they do not provide a complete year-by-year personnel record or an exhaustive list of every task she performed. Those details should not be inferred from unspecific online biographies.

Sources: [NASA biography](https://science.nasa.gov/people/adriana-ocampo/); [UNESCO COMEST directory](https://www.unesco.org/fr/ethics-science-technology/comest?hl=es-PE).

## From research to science-program leadership

Later in her career, Ocampo held science-program management and program-executive responsibilities at NASA Headquarters. NASA's Spanish-language profile from 2022 describes her as a Science Program Manager in NASA's Science Mission Directorate, Planetary Science Division, and states she led three New Frontiers missions: New Horizons, Juno, and OSIRIS-REx. A United Nations space-cooperation meeting document similarly records her as the New Frontiers Lead Program Executive, with responsibility for those missions, and notes Venus-science coordination involving NASA's collaboration with ESA's Venus Express and Japan's Akatsuki.

The words “led” and “responsible for” in program-management profiles refer to managing program selection, funding, planning, execution, and institutional coordination. They should not be converted into a claim that one program executive personally designed or carried out every instrument, observation, discovery, and engineering operation. Each mission has a large distributed team and specific principal investigators, project managers, mission operations teams, contractors, and partner institutions.

Sources: [NASA en Español biography (29 March 2022)](https://ciencia.nasa.gov/gente-de-la-nasa/dra-adriana-ocampo-uria-explorando-el-universo-e-inspirando-en-la-tierra/); [UNOOSA document on planetary science cooperation (2022 PDF)](https://www.unoosa.org/documents/pdf/copuos/stsc/2022/APSCOSymposiumDuringSTSC-ProgramOutline10Feb2022.pdf).

## Scientific identity and the Chicxulub impact structure

One of Ocampo's most prominent scientific contributions was to the interpretation of the Chicxulub impact structure beneath Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula. The buried crater is associated with the end-Cretaceous mass extinction. Her work with collaborators helped connect a ring of sinkholes known as cenotes, mapped with Landsat imagery, to the surface expression of the buried crater. The peer-reviewed paper by Kevin O. Pope, Adriana C. Ocampo, and Charles E. Duller published in 1993 provides an important contemporary scientific source. A 1996 paper by Pope and Ocampo further analyzed the crater's surface expression.

The phrasing matters: the Chicxulub structure was a collaborative scientific investigation involving geophysics, geological mapping, remote sensing, fieldwork, and a wider scientific debate about the end-Cretaceous extinction. Describing Ocampo's role as a central contribution to recognizing and characterizing the cenote ring and the impact structure is well supported. Claiming she alone “discovered the crater” would erase collaborators and the accumulation of evidence by multiple teams.

Sources: [Pope, Ocampo & Duller (1993), peer-reviewed article](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00575099); [NASA biography](https://science.nasa.gov/people/adriana-ocampo/); [PubMed record for the 1996 paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11539331/).

## Current professional description: caution required

As of the research date, the official records are not perfectly synchronized:

- NASA Science's public biography still labels her “Scientist — NASA Headquarters.” NASA's page metadata says it was last updated on 5 November 2024.
- UNESCO's COMEST directory, last updated 25 September 2026, explicitly labels the NASA program-management posts as former positions, and lists her as a UCF Courtesy Research Professor, founder and president of the Stelpolvo Ocampo Uria Foundation, member of the Colombian Academy of Exact, Physical and Natural Sciences, and Vice-President of COMEST for 2026–2027.
- UCF's faculty directory independently lists her as Courtesy Research Professor.

For that reason, the safest current formulation is: **Ocampo is a former NASA planetary-science program executive whose publicly listed current roles include Courtesy Research Professor at UCF and Vice-President of UNESCO COMEST for 2026–2027.** The NASA bio is still valuable as a first-person career source, but its job title should not override newer records.

Sources: [NASA profile and update date](https://science.nasa.gov/people/adriana-ocampo/); [UNESCO COMEST directory](https://www.unesco.org/fr/ethics-science-technology/comest?hl=es-PE); [UCF faculty profile](https://sciences.ucf.edu/physics/person/adriana-c-ocampo-uria/).

## Why her career matters

Ocampo's professional path illustrates several forms of scientific contribution:

1. **Research:** geologic interpretation of impact structures using satellite and radar imagery, supported by field evidence.
2. **Mission execution:** connecting scientific goals with practical program planning and oversight for multiple planetary missions.
3. **International cooperation:** working across NASA, JPL, ESA, Japan's space agency and later UNESCO's international ethics forum.
4. **Representation and outreach:** speaking to the aspirations and structural barriers encountered by women and Latin Americans in space science.
5. **Institution-building:** the current UNESCO listing of her foundation and academic role suggests continued involvement in STEM education and public-interest science beyond her NASA role.
